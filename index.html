Our Story

A Modest Bistro
with Serious Intent

"We make British food with a classic or modern slant and source our ingredients from the local fields, woodlands and waters."

We use free range eggs, milk and cream from farms up the road. We serve a selection of local marsh lamb and pork, and fresh fish landed just a few hundred metres from us — the gurnard, whose tail graces our sign, was caught in Rye Bay itself.

You'll start your meal with our own sourdough bread. Late every evening we set a 'starter' to froth naturally, and the morning after, flour is added. During the day it proves slowly and comes steaming hot from the oven the moment the restaurant opens.

We make all the puddings ourselves — pastry, ice cream and sorbets included. Real cream, butter, free range eggs, fresh fruit, real vanilla and quality chocolate. No mixes, no factory bases. We are passionate about food. We know you are too.

In Our Kitchen

Where It All Starts

Stocks slowly simmering in pots, meats and fish being slow-cooked, sauces reducing in the pans, vegetables gently cooked to order. Our food is British, with a classic or modern slant. Some bistro favourites stay on the menu all year, while other dishes change with the seasons.

Our fish is what was genuinely caught in nets by fishermen off the south coast — Lemon Sole, Cod, Scallops, Squid and the odd Gurnard depending on season and weather. Our forager brings his latest finds of mushrooms, wild herbs and berries all year round.

The lamb on our menu has lived off the slightly salty grass of the Romney Marsh, giving the meat a unique flavour. Our game — rabbit, venison, pigeon or mallard — has roamed the local woodlands. Martin, our chef, even grows some herbs, quinces, berries and edible flowers on his allotment and in our back garden.

Head Chef

Martin Peacock

"Having a restaurant and a chance to cook felt like a rebirth."

Martin has always liked cooking — even as a kid. It's part greed, but also the challenge of getting it right, other people's enjoyment, and the satisfaction of working with his hands. Before the restaurant, Martin spent 27 years as a university lecturer — a career he enjoyed. But cooking felt like something else entirely.

The restaurant, like the rest of the economy, has faced its challenges. Martin himself has overcome the effects of a severe stroke. But still, when the candles are lit and they're producing unpretentious, reasonably priced food for appreciative customers — he feels lucky.
